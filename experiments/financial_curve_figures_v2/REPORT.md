## 1. Why the earlier log-price plot hid the model separation

A log price axis devotes most of its vertical space to the deep out-of-the-money region, where the
call is worth a fraction of a currency unit and the models differ by tiny absolute amounts that look
large in logarithm. The economically relevant region — near the money, where the option carries its
time value — is compressed into a few percent of the figure. The log figures are diagnostics, not
presentation, and they are not used here.

## 2. Why the new primary figures use a linear price axis

A linear axis shows the actual money at stake. Model separation is then shown where it belongs, in a
**residual panel** underneath: C_model(S) − C_DH_exact(S) on a linear axis centred at zero. The price
panel stays untouched, and the residual panel resolves differences of 0.001 on prices of order 10.

## 3. Why C(S) must be increasing and convex

A European call's payoff max(S−K,0) is non-decreasing and convex in S, and discounted expectation
preserves both under any arbitrage-free measure. So ∂C/∂S ∈ [0,1] and ∂²C/∂S² ≥ 0. A violation would
mean a static arbitrage (a bull spread or a butterfly with negative cost and non-negative payoff).

**Measured on every plotted curve** (30 curve checks, finite-difference delta and gamma):

- delta violations: **0**, gamma violations: **0**
- prices below intrinsic: **0**, prices above S: **0**

Details in `data/shape_checks.json`.

## 4. Why C(τ) and C(t) are different objects

τ = T − t is **remaining** maturity; t is **calendar** time. For a non-dividend European call with
non-negative rates, more remaining time is worth more, so C(τ) is **increasing** in τ (verified:
0 violations in 399 steps on the exact Double Heston ATM curve). Holding the option and letting the
calendar advance means τ shrinks, so C(t) **decreases** toward the payoff. They are the same function
traversed in opposite directions, and calling t "time to maturity" would invert the economics.

## 5. The hand-drawn declining curve is calendar time

`C_vs_calendar_time_to_expiry.png` reproduces that shape honestly: x is calendar time t running from
0 to T = 730 days, with τ = T − t annotated on the figure, S = 105, K = 100. As t → T the three exact
models converge to the payoff: terminal gap **0.0000** for Black-Scholes, Single Heston and exact
Double Heston. The PINN curve stops at τ = 7 days, the edge of its trained domain, and the figure
marks that line rather than extrapolating.

## 6. Why the controlled benchmark is separate from market evidence

The controlled benchmark answers "what does the two-factor structure do?" It is generated by exact
Double Heston, so Double Heston is correct **by construction** there; that is a statement about
representation, not about markets. The market figures answer a different question — do these fixed
published parameters price real quotes well — and they are labelled and reported separately. The
earlier scan across 16 real surfaces found the Double-Heston-versus-Single-Heston difference to be
0.0–0.3 vol points, and that result is unchanged and not hidden by anything here.

## 7. Where Single Heston and Black-Scholes differ from exact Double Heston

Largest absolute price gap over S ∈ [0.70K, 1.30K], with K = 100:

| τ (days) | Black-Scholes | Single Heston | DH-PINN | SH gap ÷ PINN gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 30 | 0.110 | 0.117 | 8.8e-4 | 133× |
| 90 | 0.211 | 0.135 | 9.5e-4 | 142× |
| 180 | 0.255 | 0.082 | 6.0e-4 | 136× |
| 365 | 0.254 | 0.040 | 1.4e-3 | 28× |
| 730 | 0.190 | 0.131 | 2.1e-3 | 62× |

The residuals are **structured, not noise**: both one-factor models are too expensive below the money
and too cheap above it at 90 days, crossing near S = 98 — a systematic smirk-slope error. On the ATM
term structure the Single Heston gap peaks at **25 days** (0.062), exactly where the fast factor
(κ_f = 10.75, half-life ≈ 24 days) is still active and a single mean-reversion rate cannot match both
ends. Where curves cross, the figures show the crossing; no ordering was imposed.

## 8. How closely the improved PINN follows exact Double Heston

On the shared 70 × 70 surface grid (S ∈ [70, 130], τ ∈ [7, 730] days):

- **max |C_PINN − C_DH| = 2.12e-3**, at S = 130, τ = 730 days (the corner of the domain)
- **RMSE = 4.77e-4**, **P95 = 1.11e-3**
- for comparison, max |C_SH − C_DH| = **0.139** and max |C_BS − C_DH| = **0.261**

The PINN is 65–130× closer to exact Double Heston than the strongest one-factor alternative.
`advantage_surface.png` plots |C_SH − C_DH| − |C_PINN − C_DH|: it is positive on **95.1%** of the grid.
The negative 4.9% sits in the deep out-of-the-money corner where all three models are within about
1e-3 of each other and the comparison is numerically meaningless.

**Stated precisely:** Black-Scholes provides the standard option-pricing reference shape. Exact Double
Heston is the PINN's numerical pricing target. The PINN is not claimed to follow Black-Scholes.

## 9. Where the differences are largest across S and τ

- **In S:** near the money and slightly below it. The gap is ~0 in both tails, where every model is
  pinned by the payoff and the upper bound.
- **In τ:** for Single Heston, short and intermediate maturities (peak near 25 days), because that is
  the fast factor's timescale; for Black-Scholes, intermediate to long maturities, because a flat
  volatility cannot bend to any term structure.
- **`fast_vs_slow_same_total_variance.png` is the mechanism figure.** Both states have
  v_f + v_s = 0.04, so instantaneous volatility is 20% in both. Yet:

  | τ | ATM implied vol, fast-heavy | slow-heavy | ATM call price |
  |---|---:|---:|---|
  | 30 d | 19.84% | 22.10% | 2.290 vs 2.550 |
  | 90 d | 19.95% | 23.77% | 3.958 vs 4.714 |
  | 365 d | 21.12% | 24.77% | 8.421 vs 9.868 |
  | 730 d | 22.10% | 24.70% | 12.417 vs 13.866 |

  Identical variance **today**, materially different prices at every maturity, because variance sitting
  in the fast factor decays at κ_f = 10.75 while variance in the slow factor persists at κ_s = 0.95, a
  ratio of 11.3×. **No single-factor model can produce both curves from the same instantaneous
  variance.** That is the representational advantage, shown as a mechanism rather than asserted.

## 10. Financial-shape violations

None. Across all 30 plotted curves: zero delta violations, zero gamma violations, zero prices below
intrinsic value, zero prices above S, zero calendar-arbitrage violations in the ATM term structure,
and exact convergence to the payoff as t → T.

## Market figures (Type 1 — real quotes, no claim of a Double Heston win)

Surface: **SPY**, fixed by the frozen rule in `dh_pinn_v5/multi_surface/RULE.md` (largest failure of a
single-timescale term structure), chosen before any model error was seen.

- `market_SPY_C_vs_S_with_error.png` — market quotes, four models, and the pricing error underneath.
  Expiry 2026-12-18 (85 days): price RMSE **Black-Scholes 2.44, Single Heston 1.73, Double Heston 1.63,
  DH-PINN 1.63**. Here Double Heston is slightly better.
- `market_SPY_IV_error_by_moneyness.png` — implied-vol error across the whole surface. Over all 2,975
  quotes: **Black-Scholes 7.27, Single Heston 6.51, Double Heston 6.54, DH-PINN 7.01** vol points.
  **On this surface Single Heston is slightly better than Double Heston overall**, and that is shown
  rather than hidden.
- The step in the error panel near S ≈ 771 is the market's own call/put crossover at the money, where
  the quote set switches from out-of-the-money puts to out-of-the-money calls; it is data, not a model
  artifact.

The honest summary: the controlled benchmark demonstrates the two-factor mechanism and the PINN's
fidelity to it; the market figures show that with fixed published parameters, that mechanism does not
translate into a material market advantage on today's surfaces.
